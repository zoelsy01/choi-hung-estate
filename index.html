03 THE PROPOSAL

One new chapter.
Four clear rules.

The chapter applies to TV and radio licensees and RTHK. It regulates how content is made and disclosed—not what a programme says.

It covers both AI-enhanced work (AI helps a person) and AI-powered work (AI makes the content itself), because both reach an audience.

01ALL GENRES · TIERED

Tell the viewer.

Use a clear label whenever AI generates or materially alters images, video, voice or a presenter.

“Material” means: it changes what a reasonable viewer would believe happened, who spoke or what they said.

Routine colour correction, noise removal and checked transcription do not need a label.

02NEWS & CURRENT AFFAIRS

No synthetic reality in news.

AI-generated footage must never be presented as actual footage of a news event. AI illustrations are allowed only when clearly labelled.

Real people: no synthetic face or voice of an identifiable person without consent and a clear label.

Any AI presenter must be identified as AI.

03NEWS · FACTUAL

A human signs off.

Every AI-assisted news, current-affairs or factual item has a named responsible editor, recorded in a sign-off log.

Keep the record: the sign-off log is retained for 12 months and made available to the Authority on request.

A person checks AI subtitles and translations before broadcast.

04ALL LICENSEES & RTHK

Publish your AI policy.

Each station publishes its AI-use policy and files an annual AI transparency report with the CA.

Report: programme types, main AI tools and vendors, and AI-related corrections.

A public record lets audiences and researchers check the claims.

THE PRINCIPLE

Disclosure should follow the risk of misleading people—not the mere presence of a tool.

See the label tiers →

04 DIFFERENT GENRES · DIFFERENT WEIGHT

One principle.
Four label tiers.

The more a synthetic element could change a viewer’s understanding, the more prominent and persistent the disclosure should be.

PROPOSED DISCLOSURE

News & current affairs

Keep the on-screen label visible for as long as the AI-made material appears. A named editor remains on file.

05 THE WORDING

A draft chapter
built for Hong Kong.

The proposed tests are about method: was AI used, was it labelled, and who signed off? The CA does not judge whether a programme’s viewpoint is acceptable.

Read the source Codes ↗
PROPOSED TEXTNEW CHAPTER · ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

A licensee shall clearly inform the audience when artificial intelligence has been used to generate or materially alter any image, video, voice or presenter in a programme.

In news and current affairs programmes, that information shall be shown on screen for as long as the material appears, in a form easy to notice and understand.

A licensee shall not present material generated by artificial intelligence as actual footage or a recording of a news event.

A licensee shall not broadcast a synthetic image or voice of an identifiable real person without that person’s consent and a clear label, except where the material is itself the subject of a labelled news report.

For each AI-assisted news, current affairs or factual item, a licensee shall name a responsible editor and keep a record for 12 months, available to the Authority on request.

A licensee shall publish its AI-use policy and submit an annual report to the Authority in the form it specifies.

06 WHEN CLIPS TRAVEL

Make the label
travel with it.

An on-screen note can disappear when a clip is cut, reposted or stripped from its original programme. Provenance metadata offers a second layer.

A TECHNICAL LAYER · NEWS FIRST

Pilot C2PA Content Credentials.

From 2028, stations voluntarily pilot C2PA on news clips they post online. The open standard can attach verifiable information about a clip’s origin and editing history—helping viewers and platforms check whether a clip really came from the claimed broadcaster.

France Télévisions signs its daily news; Fuji TV has used C2PA on air since June 2025; Ukraine’s public broadcaster Suspilne built a system with a team of three in about six months.16

08 ACCOUNTABILITY

A points ladder,
not a cliff edge.

Use sanctions the CA already has under the Broadcasting Ordinance. Add a published points guide, so stations know how repeat breaches escalate.

  1. 01
    1 POINT

    Advice or strong advice

    First minor breach—for example, a label that is too small or too brief.

  2. 02
    2–3 POINTS

    Warning or serious warning

    A repeat breach within 24 months, or a missing disclosure in news.

  3. 03
    4 POINTS

    On-air correction or apology

    Synthetic footage passed off as real news footage; correct the record on the same channel.

  4. 04
    6+ POINTS

    Financial penalty

    Existing caps: HK$200,000 first; HK$400,000 second; HK$1,000,000 after that.

  5. 05
    EXISTING POWERS

    Suspension or revocation

    Existing powers only: suspension of up to 30 days in cases set out in law; revocation by the Chief Executive in Council. A last resort—no new power is created.

WHY NOT REVOKE FIRST?

Protect the public’s right to know.

If all three free-TV stations refused at once, pulling licences would punish viewers too. Penalties should first target corporate money and reputation while the public keeps its licensed channels.

In 2016, the CA moved to suspend ATV’s licence after repeated failures. That precedent shows revocation is an existing last resort—not the first response.

10 SCOPE & SAFEGUARDS

Start where
the law reaches.

Licensed broadcasters use public resources and accept licence conditions, so the CA can act through existing codes. Broader online rules need a different path.

PHASE 1 · NOW

Licensed broadcasters

Apply the chapter to TV and radio licensees and RTHK through the Codes of Practice. These are institutions the CA already regulates.

PHASE 2 · VOLUNTARY

Online outlets

Print, online outlets and self-media are not licensed in the same way and are difficult to define. Binding duties need new law; a voluntary template can move through the Digital Policy Office’s sector guidelines.

THE SAFEGUARD

Method, not message

Every test is factual: Was AI used? Was it labelled? Is an editor named? No pre-approval of stories and no judgment about a programme’s viewpoint.

A REGIONAL DIRECTION

Hong Kong can catch up without overreaching.

Comparable moves pair AI adoption with transparency and human accountability.

EU

AI Act Article 50 includes transparency duties for deepfakes, applying from 2 August 2026.12

KOREA

The AI Basic Act introduces duties related to disclosure of generative and deepfake content.10

MAINLAND

NRTA rules announced on 17 September 2026 pair AI programme labels with human review.11

11 THE STRESS TEST

Hard questions.
Direct answers.

These are the objections raised in the group’s peer stress test and the questions a policymaker panel is likely to ask.

＋ Select a question to read the answer.

01TV is losing viewers and money. Why regulate AI instead of helping stations use it?

We do both. Clear rules make AI safer to use. TVB has said AI could reduce long-form production costs by 20–30%; the Mainland regulator’s approach pairs encouragement with labels and human review. A station that hides AI and is caught damages trust in the licensed system.

02If all three stations ignore the rules, do you take their licences away?

No—not first. Advice, warnings, an on-air correction and fines can escalate up to existing caps. Revocation is a last resort under existing law. The worst case is the public losing free, licensed news; viewers’ right to know matters too.

03Who enforces the chapter—and with what power?

The Communications Authority, through the Codes of Practice. Licensees already follow the Codes, and RTHK is bound under its Charter. No new regulator and no new law: a new chapter plus a published points guide.

04What stops a station from complying only on paper?

Spot checks compare the sign-off log with what actually aired. Annual reports are public. A false report should earn more points than a missing label: the paper must match the screen.

05Is this a press-freedom risk?

The rules are designed around method, not message: was AI used, was it labelled, and who signed off? There is no pre-approval and the CA does not decide what a story may say. Provenance can also help show when a fake “TVB report” is not genuine.

06Why not cover newspapers and online media as well?

Start where existing law reaches. Print and self-media do not hold broadcast licences and are harder to define. Phase 2 offers the chapter as a voluntary template through sector guidelines.

07Won’t labels everywhere cause label fatigue? Can’t the CA just use AI detectors?

That is why labels are tiered: routine edits need none; drama needs a notice before the programme and in credits. AI detectors are not the proposed safeguard. The EBU/BBC study found 45% of AI assistant answers about news had at least one significant issue—evidence for human review, not a measure of detector accuracy.25 The proposal relies on records, named editors, reports and spot checks instead.

08What if the proposal is wrong?

Build in a way back. A 12-month audience survey and the 2030 review can simplify the tiers if labels confuse people or stop useful AI. A Code is easier to amend than a new law.

12 METHOD & COURSE GROUNDING

Show the evidence.
Name the limits.

A policy pitch should be actionable, grounded in course thinking and honest about what its evidence cannot establish.

01 · METHOD

Public-source desk audit

Four broadcasters; public documents and reporting checked on 15 November 2026. We compared documented AI uses with publicly available AI-use policies.

02 · LIMIT

Absence is not proof

“Not found” describes this search, not what may exist internally. A desk audit cannot establish frequency of use or prove how every on-air disclosure is handled.

03 · FRAMEWORK

Make hidden systems visible

Roberts’ work on hidden platform labour and Crawford’s account of AI’s material power inform why the supply chain and its human checks should be legible.

04 · SAFEGUARD

Human oversight & verification

Wishart’s journalism ethics guidance supports named human review. The Ladder of Verification pushes “we use AI responsibly” from a claim toward something others can check.
